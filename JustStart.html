THE JOURNAL / GETTING STARTED

Your first planted aquarium, made simple.

A calm, practical guide to creating a healthy freshwater world—from the first glass box to the first quiet moment of wonder.

By Rowan Ellis · Frn's aquarist · October 8, 2026 · 8 min read

A thriving aquarium is built in layers: a thoughtful layout, healthy plants, and time.

You don’t need to know everything to begin. You just need a few good essentials, a little patience, and an understanding of what makes an aquarium feel like home to the life inside it.

YOUR STARTING POINT
Aquarium 40–60 L is a forgiving first size
Lighting Start at 6 hours daily
Before adding fish 0 ppm ammonia · 0 ppm nitrite
Temperature Match your species, not a universal number

01. Give your world a good foundation.

Start with a 40–60 liter aquarium if you have the space. A little more water makes temperature and chemistry easier to keep stable. Place it on a level, load-rated stand away from direct sunlight, heaters, and drafts. Once filled, an aquarium is much heavier than it looks.

Rinse the empty tank with clean water—never soap. Add a protective leveling mat if your aquarium requires one. Before you build your landscape, plan where the filter, heater, and light will sit, and leave room to reach them for maintenance.

02. Build a landscape that can grow.

Arrange your substrate, rocks, and aquarium-safe wood before adding water. Use a nutrient-rich aquarium soil for rooted plants, following the manufacturer's instructions. Fill slowly over a plate or bag so you don't disturb your layout. Treat tap water with an aquarium dechlorinator.

For a forgiving first planting, try Anubias Nana Petite or Java Fern Trident. Attach their rhizomes to wood or rock; burying them can cause rot. Begin with six hours of light each day on a timer. Increase gradually only if your plants need it, and reduce the duration if algae begins to take over.

03. Let the invisible world establish.

A clear tank is not necessarily a ready tank. Beneficial bacteria need time to grow in your filter and convert toxic ammonia into nitrite, then less harmful nitrate. This is the nitrogen cycle, and it is the most important step before welcoming any fish.

Cycle without fish using an aquarium-safe ammonia source and a reliable liquid test kit. Keep the filter running around the clock and follow a fishless-cycling method carefully. It commonly takes four to six weeks, sometimes longer. Your tank is ready only when a measured ammonia dose is processed to zero ammonia and zero nitrite within 24 hours. Do a water change to lower nitrate before adding fish.

04. Add life slowly, and thoughtfully.

Choose fish for your actual tank size, temperature, water hardness, and each other’s temperament—not just their color. Research the adult size and social needs of every species. Cardinal tetras need a proper school and a mature, suitably sized aquarium. A male betta should never share a tank with another male betta.

Add a small number of compatible fish at a time, and check ammonia and nitrite daily while the bacteria adjust. Quarantine new arrivals in a separate, cycled setup where possible. Follow the seller’s species-specific acclimation instructions, keep the lights low, and never pour shipping water into your aquarium.

05. Make sure you have a quiet weekly ritual.

Spend a few minutes each day watching your aquarium. Are the fish active? Is the filter flowing? Is the temperature steady? Feed small portions appropriate to your species, removing uneaten food. More food is rarely more love.

Each week, test your water and change roughly 20–30%, adjusting to your stocking and test results. Use dechlorinated, temperature-matched water. Trim plants and gently clean the glass. Rinse filter sponges in removed tank water, not untreated tap water, and don’t replace all biological media at once. A stable aquarium rewards consistency more than constant tinkering.

Your water, your pace.

There’s no single perfect schedule for every aquarium. Keep notes, test regularly, and ask us when something looks different. We’re here to help.
